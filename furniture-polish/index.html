Furniture polish without silicone: how to choose the right one

Spray polish, cream, oil or wax all promise a shine. They work in very different ways, and the choice affects how your furniture looks years from now.

By Oakmere Wood Care. Updated November 2026.

“Furniture polish” covers a wide range of products. Some clean, some add a shine that lasts until the next dusting, and some build up a protective layer over time. Knowing which is which saves you from the most common problems: sticky build-up, cloudy patches and finishes that are hard to repair later.

The main types of furniture polish

Aerosol and spray polishes

Quick and convenient, and good at picking up dust. Many contain silicone oils that give an instant, glossy shine. The shine sits on top of the finish and wears off quickly, so sprays are usually used often, and the residue can build up into a smeary film.

Liquid oil polishes

Oil-based polishes darken and refresh dry-looking wood. On their own they add little protection, and too much oil left on the surface stays tacky and attracts dust.

Paste wax and beeswax balms

Wax-based polish is applied less often but lasts longer. A thin buffed layer gives a soft, hand-rubbed glow and helps the surface resist light moisture and fingerprints. A balm that combines wax with oil, like Oakmere Beeswax Wood Polish & Conditioner, also conditions dry wood in the same step.

Why silicone-free matters

Silicone gives a fast shine, but it can soak into the finish and the wood beneath it. If a piece ever needs to be repaired or refinished, leftover silicone can stop new finish from bonding evenly and cause small craters known as “fish eye”. Furniture restorers often ask what a piece has been polished with for exactly this reason. A silicone-free wax keeps that door open, which matters most for antiques and furniture you plan to keep for decades.

How to choose

  • For dusting between polishes: a dry or barely damp microfibre cloth is all you need.
  • For dull or dry-looking wood: a beeswax balm with oil conditions and polishes in one step.
  • For high-touch surfaces: tables, arms of chairs and stair railings benefit from a buffed wax layer.
  • For antiques and heirlooms: choose silicone-free, and test a hidden spot first.

How often to polish

Less often than most people think. Wax-polishing every few months is plenty for most furniture, and once or twice a year is fine for pieces that are rarely handled. Polish again when the surface looks pale, feels dry, or water stops beading. In between, simply dust. Over-polishing is the most common cause of a cloudy, sticky finish; our wood care myths page explains why.

What not to polish

Do not wax wood floors, which can become slippery. Skip painted surfaces unless the manufacturer allows wax, and avoid fresh finishes that have not fully cured. For everything else, start with a small test area and a thin coat.
