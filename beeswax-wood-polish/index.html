Beeswax wood polish, wood wax and wood conditioner, explained

Three names that often describe the same tin. Here is what each one means, what beeswax actually does for wood, and how to get a good result.

By Oakmere Wood Care. Updated November 2026.

Walk down the cleaning aisle and you will see “wood polish”, “wood wax”, “wood conditioner” and “furniture balm” on products that look almost identical. The labels overlap because the products overlap. Most beeswax-based wood care is a blend of two things: a wax that sits in the surface and protects it, and an oil that carries the wax into the grain and keeps the wood from looking dry.

What each term usually means

TermWhat it usually isWhat it is good for
Wood polishAnything that leaves a sheen when buffed: sprays, creams, pastes or balmsBringing back lustre on furniture that looks flat
Wood waxA paste of beeswax, carnauba or both, often softened with oilA protective, buffable layer that resists light moisture and fingerprints
Wood conditionerAn oil, or an oil-rich wax blend, that soaks into the woodWood that looks pale, dry or thirsty
Beeswax balmA soft wax-and-oil blend that spreads like a thick creamDoing all three jobs in one step

A balm like Oakmere Beeswax Wood Polish & Conditioner sits in that last row. The oil conditions, the wax protects, and buffing gives the polish.

What beeswax does for wood

Beeswax has been used on furniture for centuries because it is easy to work with. It softens with the warmth of your hand, spreads thinly, and buffs to a soft, low glow rather than a plastic-looking shine. Once buffed, it leaves a thin layer that helps the surface shrug off light moisture and everyday handling. It also fills tiny scratches and dull patches in the finish, which is a large part of why old pieces look richer after a coat.

Why carnauba is added

Carnauba is a plant wax that is much harder than beeswax. A small amount in the blend gives a firmer, longer-lasting sheen and makes the buffed surface feel less tacky. Too much makes a paste hard to spread, which is why Oakmere uses only a little: the balm stays soft and easy to glide on, but buffs to a crisper finish than beeswax alone.

Why food-grade mineral oil

The oil in a wood balm needs to stay stable. Cooking oils such as olive or vegetable oil can oxidise in the wood over time and turn sticky or smell stale. Food-grade mineral oil (USP) does not go rancid, has no scent, and soaks into dry wood well. Oakmere uses it in every product.

How to get a good result

  • Clean first. Wax traps whatever is on the surface, so dust and wipe away grime before you start.
  • Go thin. A little goes a long way. Thick coats stay soft and sticky.
  • Give it time. Leave the balm for 10 to 20 minutes so the oil can soak in, then buff.
  • Buff properly. Use a clean, soft cloth and buff until the surface feels smooth and dry, not greasy.

The full routine is in our guide on how to polish wood furniture.

Where beeswax polish works, and where it does not

Use it on solid wood and on wood with a finish that is intact: tables, dressers, bed frames, cabinets, doors, trim, railings and antiques. It also works on raw, unfinished wood. Avoid using wax on floors, because it can make them slippery, and test a hidden spot first on antiques, painted pieces or any finish you are unsure about. If the finish is peeling or worn through to grey wood, polish will improve the look but cannot replace a finish; see how to restore dry wood for how to tell the difference.
